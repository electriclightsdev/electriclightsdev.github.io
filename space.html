Red Moon Over Earth

Soviet satellite circles the globe every 96 minutes — beeps heard on amateur radios worldwide

— SPUTNIK 1, seen before launch at Baikonur Cosmodrome

By Reuters Wire  ·  Moscow

The Soviet Union announced last night that it has successfully placed an artificial satellite in orbit around the Earth. The object, designated SPUTNIK 1, weighs 83.6 kilograms and completes a full revolution of the planet once every 96 minutes at an altitude of approximately 577 miles.

The launch took place at 19:28 GMT on October 4th from the Tyuratam range in Kazakhstan atop a modified R-7 ballistic missile. Radio operators across Europe and North America have confirmed hearing the satellite's characteristic 20 MHz pulse as it passes overhead.

The device is expected to remain in orbit for approximately three months before atmospheric drag causes its descent. The American response has thus far been limited. The Eisenhower administration declined comment. Senator Lyndon Johnson has requested emergency hearings.

Чайка: A Woman in Space

Valentina Tereshkova completes 48 orbits aboard Vostok 6

Junior Lieutenant Valentina Vladimirovna Tereshkova, 26, a textile worker and amateur parachutist from Maslennikovo, has returned to Earth after spending 70 hours and 50 minutes in orbit — longer than every American astronaut combined to date. Her call sign was "Чайка" — Seagull.

She landed in Kazakhstan on 19 June, was promptly offered bread and salt by a collective farm, and reportedly refused medical attention in favor of sharing her rations with onlookers.

One Giant Leap

20 July 1969  //  Apollo 11

The New York Times
Vol. CXVIII  ·  Monday, July 21, 1969  ·  Late City Edition  ·  Ten Cents

Men Walk on Moon

Astronauts Land on Plain; Collect Rocks, Plant Flag

— BUZZ ALDRIN photographed by NEIL ARMSTRONG, Sea of Tranquility

By JOHN NOBLE WILFORD  ·  Special to The Times  ·  Houston

Men have landed and walked on the moon. Two Americans, astronauts of Apollo 11, steered their fragile four-legged lunar module safely and smoothly to the historic landing yesterday at 4:17:40 P.M., Eastern daylight time. Neil A. Armstrong, the 38-year-old civilian commander, radioed to earth and the mission control room here: "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed."

The first men to reach the moon — Mr. Armstrong and his co-pilot, Col. Edwin E. Aldrin Jr. of the Air Force — brought their ship to rest on a level, rock-strewn plain near the southwestern shore of the arid Sea of Tranquility. About six and a half hours later, Mr. Armstrong opened the landing craft's hatch, stepped slowly down the ladder and declared as he planted the first human footprint on the lunar crust: "That's one small step for a man, one giant leap for mankind."

His first step on the moon came at 10:56:20 P.M. Mr. Aldrin joined him on the moon's surface 19 minutes later. Michael Collins, 38, an Air Force lieutenant colonel, remained in lunar orbit in the command module Columbia, circling 70 miles overhead. The two moon explorers spent 2 hours and 21 minutes outside. They deployed a television camera, collected 47.5 pounds of lunar rock and soil, planted the flag, and spoke briefly with President Nixon.

The Eagle lifted off from the moon at 1:54 P.M. today, Eastern daylight time, after 21 hours and 36 minutes on the lunar surface. Splashdown in the Pacific is scheduled for Thursday.

Looking Back in Time

The Hubble Deep Field shows us 3,000 galaxies in a patch of sky the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length

— THE HUBBLE DEEP FIELD, 342 exposures stacked, 10 days of observing

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched aboard STS-31 on 24 April 1990, was nearly a catastrophe. Its primary mirror, ground to the wrong specification, rendered early images blurry. A 1993 servicing mission installed corrective optics. Since then it has operated as perhaps the most productive scientific instrument in history.

Between 18 and 28 December 1995, astronomer Robert Williams pointed the telescope at a dark patch of sky in Ursa Major — a region chosen specifically because it appeared empty. What the instrument returned has redrawn the map of creation.

They Came Back

Artemis II crew splashes down after 10-day lunar flyby — first humans beyond low Earth orbit in 54 years

— ORION SPACECRAFT "INTEGRITY" during lunar flyby, 6 April 2026

By Staff Reporters  ·  Ellington Airport, Houston

Four astronauts walked off a recovery flight at Ellington Airport this afternoon, ten days after becoming the first humans to travel beyond low Earth orbit since the crew of Apollo 17 departed the Moon in December 1972.

Commander Reid Wiseman, Pilot Victor Glover, Mission Specialist Christina Koch, and CSA astronaut Jeremy Hansen launched aboard the Orion spacecraft — which they had named Integrity — atop a Space Launch System rocket from Kennedy Space Center on 1 April 2026. On 6 April they executed a seven-hour flyby of the Moon's far side, passing within 8,890 km of the lunar surface.

Koch is now the woman who has flown furthest from Earth; Glover the first Black astronaut to have left low Earth orbit; Hansen the first non-American to do so. A small plush toy — "Rise," a moon wearing Earth as a baseball cap, inspired by the Apollo 8 Earthrise photograph — accompanied them and returned with the crew.

Artemis III, targeted for late 2027, is now expected to perform the first crewed lunar landing in over 55 years.
